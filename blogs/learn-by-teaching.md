You have read the chapter twice. You highlighted it. You nodded along in class. Then a friend asks you to explain it, and suddenly it all falls apart.

That moment is not a failure. It is the most useful signal in learning. **Learning by teaching** turns that signal into a daily habit. You study a topic so that you can explain it to someone else, and the explaining is where real understanding happens.

## What is learning by teaching?

Learning by teaching (also called *learn by teaching* or *teach to learn*) means you prepare to explain a topic to another person, then actually explain it. It sounds simple, but it changes how you study:

- You stop re-reading and start **organising** ideas into a story someone else can follow.
- You have to **recall** the material from memory instead of recognising it on the page.
- Every question your listener asks shows you a **gap** you didn't know you had.

The old Latin phrase *docendo discimus*, "by teaching, we learn", says the same thing. People have known it for centuries. Most study routines still ignore it.

## Why teaching others helps you learn

### The protégé effect

Psychologists call it the **protégé effect**: people put more effort into learning, and understand material more deeply, when they expect to teach it to someone else. Knowing that someone is counting on your explanation changes how carefully you read.

### The Feynman Technique

Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman believed you only truly understand something when you can explain it simply. His approach, now known as the **Feynman Technique**, has four steps:

1. **Pick a topic** and write its name at the top of a page.
2. **Explain it in plain words**, as if teaching a classmate who has never seen it.
3. **Find where you get stuck.** Those gaps are exactly what you still need to learn. Go back to the book for those parts only.
4. **Simplify and explain again** until the explanation flows without notes.

### Learning is social

Psychologist Albert Bandura's **Social Learning Theory** showed that people learn powerfully through interaction with others, not only through isolated study. Teaching a peer adds that social layer: a real listener, real questions and real accountability.

## Learning by teaching vs. re-reading notes

| Re-reading notes | Learning by teaching |
|---|---|
| Feels productive, but mostly recognition | Forces active recall from memory |
| Gaps stay hidden | Gaps show up the moment you get stuck |
| Easy to postpone until exam week | A scheduled session with a peer keeps you consistent |
| Builds memory of the page | Builds understanding, communication and confidence |

## How to learn by teaching: a 20-minute routine

You don't need a classroom. Try this with any chapter:

1. **Slice the chapter** into small sub-topics, each small enough to teach in about 20 minutes.
2. **Prepare to teach one sub-topic.** Read, make a few key points, and work out one example.
3. **Teach it to a peer**: a friend, sibling or study partner. Ask them to interrupt with doubts.
4. **Note every question you couldn't answer** and fix those gaps the same day.
5. **Finish with a short quiz** to check you can apply the idea, not just explain it.

No listener today? Explain out loud to an empty chair or record yourself. It works better than silent re-reading, but a real person asking questions works best.

## Common questions

**Do I need to be good at a topic before I teach it?**
No. Preparing to teach is how you get good at it. The goal is not a perfect lecture. It is to find out what you don't understand yet.

**Does learning by teaching work for maths and science?**
Yes. Explaining *why* each step of a solution works is one of the best ways to catch shaky concepts in maths, physics and chemistry.

**How often should I do it?**
Short and regular beats long and rare. One 20-minute teaching session per sub-topic, spread across the week, builds a habit that doesn't depend on exam pressure.
